*Part of [The Dark Side of the API Economy](https://apievangelist.com/2026/12/17/the-dark-side-of-the-api-economy/). Act two: extract.*

Mobile is now the biggest part of the games business. Newzoo put the global games market at $201.6 billion in 2025, with mobile at $113.3 billion, about 56 percent. Most of that money does not come from selling games. It comes from free games that earn through ads and in-game purchases.

That business model is where this story lives. In December 2022 Epic Games agreed to pay $520 million to settle two FTC actions. $275 million was a penalty for violating the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, which the FTC called the largest penalty ever for breaking one of its rules. The other $245 million was refunds for what the FTC described as dark patterns: an interface that made it easy for players, including children, to make purchases they did not intend.

## Loot boxes

Epic had already removed paid random-item loot boxes from Fortnite: Save the World in 2019. The wider industry has not. Belgium treats them as gambling and bans them, yet a researcher found that 82 of the 100 highest-grossing iPhone games were still selling some form of loot box.

## What the APIs show

On APIs.io, [Unity](https://apis.io/providers/unity/) publishes 33 APIs and scores exemplar. [Epic Games](https://apis.io/providers/epic-games/) publishes 23 and scores developing. [AppLovin](https://apis.io/providers/applovin/), a big part of mobile ad monetization, scores thin.

These APIs are the plumbing of free-to-play: ads, analytics, player accounts, purchases and live operations. They are well built and they make it possible for a small studio to ship a game. They also make it very efficient to measure exactly which nudge gets a player to spend, including a player who is ten years old.

## Why it matters

Nothing in a game engine's API is exploitative by itself. The harm comes from what a company chooses to optimize. When the metric is spend per player and some of the players are children, the analytics that make games better can just as easily make them more extractive. The Epic case is a reminder that regulators will look at the design, not the intent.
