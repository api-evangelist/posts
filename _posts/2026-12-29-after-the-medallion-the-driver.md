*Part of [The Dark Side of the API Economy](https://apievangelist.com/2026/12/17/the-dark-side-of-the-api-economy/). Act three: automate.*

This series started with the [taxi medallion](https://apievangelist.com/2026/12/18/the-medallion-and-the-api/), which lost about three quarters of its value once a ride request API replaced it. The people who filled the gap were gig drivers, paid by an algorithm that, as I wrote in [Personalized Pay, Personalized Price](https://apievangelist.com/2026/12/23/personalized-pay-personalized-price/), sets each driver's pay in ways they cannot see. This is the third step: removing the driver.

In February 2026 Waymo launched fully driverless service in Dallas, Houston, San Antonio and Orlando, adding to the cities where it already ran, and it is aiming for a million paid rides a week by the end of the year. Its co-CEO, Tekedra Mawakana, has said that after a few years in some markets "we haven't eliminated jobs in those markets," and that Waymo still needs people to operate, maintain and support the fleet.

Trucking is moving the same way. Aurora Innovation runs driverless freight on Texas freeways. Estimates of how many truck-driving jobs this could eventually remove range from 11,000 to 300,000, which tells you how little anyone actually knows. The industry argues that a driver shortage will soften the blow and that drivers can be retrained as technicians and dispatchers.

## What the APIs show

On APIs.io, [Waymo](https://apis.io/providers/waymo/) scores minimal and publishes no APIs at all. Aurora Innovation is not in the catalog. That is worth pausing on. The company replacing the driver exposes even less than the companies that replaced the medallion. There is no public interface for how the fleet is dispatched, priced, or how its safety is measured.

## Why it matters

Look at the three steps together. A public, regulated, visible asset (the medallion) was replaced by a private app. The workers who drove for the app were paid by a model nobody could inspect. And now the vehicle is being run by a system that publishes nothing. At each step the economics became less visible to the public.

I do not think robotaxis are bad. Many people will be safer and better served. But the question I keep coming back to in this series is the same one: who can see how the decisions are being made? Each step answered it with less.
