I spent a lot of years believing social media was the engine of developer community. Twitter was where I found my people, where I tested ideas, and where a good chunk of the API Evangelist audience came from. I don't believe that anymore, and I've changed how I spend my time because of it.

## What the numbers told me

When I look at my own analytics now, people are about 1-2% of the traffic hitting my sites. The rest is bots, crawlers and agents. The share of people arriving from social referrals has collapsed. I'd post something, it would get some likes, and almost none of that turned into someone reading the work, signing up, or submitting an API.

So earlier this year I stopped the social drips I had been running to push papers and posts out on a schedule. I didn't do it to make a statement. I did it because the effort wasn't producing anything I could measure, and the effort was real.

## Where the value actually is

What I measure as success is signups and submissions. And what moves those isn't a post on a timeline. It is content that answers a real question someone is trying to get done.

That means:

- **Use-case content.** Not "here is our API," but "here is how you get this specific thing done." A business person searching for how to automate something doesn't know an API exists. A use-case page meets them where they are.
- **Guides.** Step-by-step, technically correct, with real requests and real responses. The kind of thing someone bookmarks and comes back to.
- **YouTube.** Video keeps working for me in a way that social doesn't. People search it, it sticks around, and it explains things that are hard to explain in text.
- **Public repositories with good docs.** A well-documented repo for a hackathon, a sample app, or a workflow is something a developer can fork today. And it is something an agent can read.

## The same content serves the agents

This is the part I think a lot of developer relations teams haven't caught up to. The content that works for humans now is the same content that works for agents.

An agent answering a developer's question isn't reading your social feed. It is reading your guides, your docs, your examples and your public repos. High-quality technical content gets crawled, indexed and learned from. A clever thread disappears into a timeline in a day and was never in a shape an agent could use anyway.

When I write a use-case post, I'm writing for the person searching Google and for the agent that person is increasingly asking instead. When I publish a repo with a clear README, an OpenAPI, and a working example, I'm handing a developer something to fork and handing an agent something to reason about. One piece of work, two audiences.

## Social as a conversation channel

I'm not saying social is worthless. It is a fine place to have a conversation. I still talk with people there, answer questions, and see what folks are working on. Social works as a conversation channel. It just isn't the main community engine anymore, and I don't think it should be where a developer relations budget goes first.

The mistake I made for a while, and I see a lot of API providers still making, is measuring community by activity on social platforms. Followers, impressions and engagement feel like community. But if none of that turns into someone building on your API, it is just noise you're paying to produce.

## What I'd tell a DevRel team

If you run developer relations for an API provider and you are deciding where to put your next quarter, this is what I would do:

1. Look honestly at what your social channels produce in signups, activations and integrations, not in engagement.
2. Take the effort you'd spend on scheduled posts and put it into use-case guides for the things your customers are actually trying to do.
3. Turn your best guides into short videos.
4. Put every sample, hackathon project and reference app in a public repository with a real README, a contract and examples.
5. Keep social for conversations. Show up, answer questions, listen. Stop treating it as a broadcast channel.

The audience is still there. It is just finding you through search, through video, through repos, and more and more through agents. Make the content that shows up in all of those places, and let social be where you talk with people, not where you hope they find you.
