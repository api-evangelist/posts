*Part of [The Dark Side of the API Economy](https://apievangelist.com/2026/12/17/the-dark-side-of-the-api-economy/). Act two: extract.*

For years the case against social media and young people was a debate. In 2026 it started to become a verdict.

In March 2026 a New Mexico jury found that Meta violated the state's consumer protection law by misleading people about how safe its platforms were for teens, and ordered it to pay $375 million in civil penalties. New Mexico argued that Meta's public reassurances did not match what its own internal research said. It was the first time a state won a case like this against a major tech company. Meta says it will appeal.

The same week, a Los Angeles jury voted 10 to 2 that Meta and Google were negligent in a teen social media addiction case, finding both companies knew their designs were dangerous and did not warn users. The award was $6 million. Both companies said they would appeal.

More cases are coming. More than 40 state attorneys general have sued Meta. Unsealed filings in a school district case allege Meta stopped an internal research project, code-named Project Mercury, after it pointed to harm. Meta says the project was halted because its method was flawed.

## Where the extraction is

The product is free, so the business is attention. Features like infinite scroll and notifications are tuned to keep people on the app, and a teenager's attention is worth the same to an advertiser as an adult's. According to Tennessee's lawsuit, a 2017 internal Meta document said those features conflicted with well-being. That is the extraction: time and attention, taken from people who are still developing the ability to put the phone down.

## What the APIs show

On APIs.io, [Meta](https://apis.io/providers/meta/) publishes 19 APIs and scores strong. [Instagram](https://apis.io/providers/instagram/) publishes 9 and scores exemplar. Those APIs serve businesses, creators and advertisers. They let you publish content, manage ads and read insights about your audience.

What no API exposes is the thing the courts have been asking about: what the platform knew about its effect on young users, and what it decided to do about it. That research lives inside the company. In these cases, it came out through discovery, not documentation.

## Why it matters

The verdicts are under appeal and the larger cases are still being fought, so I am not going to say more than the juries have. But the pattern is the one running through this whole series. The interfaces a platform publishes show you what it wants you to build. The data it keeps inside shows you what it is optimizing for.
