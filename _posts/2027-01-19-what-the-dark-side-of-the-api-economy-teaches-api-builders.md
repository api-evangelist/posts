*The closing post of [The Dark Side of the API Economy](https://apievangelist.com/2026/12/17/the-dark-side-of-the-api-economy/).*

Over the last month I have written nineteen stories about how APIs have been used to reprice industries, extract value from people, and automate away the work those platforms created. I want to finish with what I think it all means for the people who design, build and buy APIs, because I am one of them.

## Three patterns

**The value moves out of sight.** The taxi medallion was a public number anyone could look up. It was replaced by a pricing algorithm, then by a pay algorithm for drivers, then by a driverless fleet that publishes nothing. In story after story, the economics became less visible each time the software got better.

**The best APIs serve the platform's side of the deal.** Some of the highest-scoring APIs in this series belong to companies whose integrations make it easy for a restaurant, a hotel or a business to plug in, and hard to take the customer back out. A great API is not the same as a fair one.

**The decisions that matter most have no interface.** How a fare is set, how a driver's pay is calculated, what a platform knew about teenagers, how a welfare system decided someone owed money. None of it was exposed. It came out through courts, regulators, researchers and journalists.

## What the scorecard misses

I score APIs for a living, and this series showed me the limits of my own scorecard. The Kin Score measures how well a company publishes its API. It does not measure whether the people in a dataset consented, whether the cancel operation exists, or whether the decision the API feeds can be explained. I am going to think hard about whether some of that belongs in how I rate APIs.

## The good side is still real

None of this changed my mind about APIs. The most hopeful stories in this series were API stories too: open banking giving people scoped, revocable control over their own data; mobile money reaching households no bank branch ever served; a court ordering a monopolist to make its systems interoperable. The tools that extract can also be used to give back.

## Three questions to ask

If you build or integrate with an API, I would ask three things.

1. After the integration, who owns the customer relationship?
2. Can the person affected by a decision see how it was made?
3. Is leaving as easy as joining?

If the answer to all three is good, you are probably on the right side of this. If not, you now know where the dark side of the API economy begins.
