I have spent sixteen years telling people that APIs are a force for good. I still believe that. An API can open up a bank account to the person who owns it, let a small restaurant own its own customer list, and give a regulator something to inspect. But I have also watched the same plumbing get used to reprice whole industries, to quietly extract value from the people on both sides of a platform, and then to automate away the workers those platforms created. I have not written about that side nearly enough.

So I am starting a series called The Dark Side of the API Economy. Each story takes one sector, one number I can cite, and the APIs that made the number possible. I map every company to its page on [APIs.io](https://apis.io) so you can see what it actually publishes, and what it does not.

The series runs in three acts.

**Reprice.** Mobile apps and the APIs behind them changed the economics of entire sectors. New York taxi medallions lost roughly three quarters of their value. A peer-reviewed study found Airbnb cut hotel revenue 8 to 10 percent in Austin. Delivery apps take 15 to 30 percent of a restaurant order. The UK lost a third of its bank branches in five years.

**Extract.** Once a platform sits between a business and its customers, it can take from both. A federal court found Google monopolized the ad tech that sits between publishers and advertisers. Amazon paid $2.5 billion over how it enrolled and kept people in Prime. Epic paid $520 million over purchases children did not mean to make. Loan apps used the contact list on your phone to shame you into paying.

**Automate.** Then the platforms automate the work they created. The gig driver who replaced the medallion owner is next in line for a robotaxi. Entry-level workers in jobs most exposed to AI have seen employment fall while their senior colleagues were spared. And underneath the AI are people in Nairobi who were paid around two dollars an hour to label the worst content on the internet.

One pattern showed up while I was mapping these companies to APIs.io, and I will come back to it. The companies doing the disrupting mostly score developing or thin on their own APIs. The infrastructure they run on, things like Twilio, Plaid, Zendesk and WhatsApp, scores exemplar. And some of the best-scored APIs in the series sell data about people. A great developer experience tells you nothing about whether the payload is ethical.

I am not anti-platform and I am not anti-automation. I want the people building with APIs to see the whole system they are part of. Every story will say clearly what is a court finding, what is a settlement, what is a forecast, and what is still an allegation.

The first story is about the taxi medallion and the API that replaced it.
