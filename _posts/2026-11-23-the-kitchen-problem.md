I think of my job a lot like being the person who wants to talk about your kitchen. Not the food. Not the coffee. The kitchen. I'm the guy who is still talking about 240-volt and 110-volt wiring and what gauge the plumbing is. Nobody gives a shit about any of that. They want the microwave to work. They want the coffee pot to work.

That's the kitchen problem, and I've been living inside it for most of my career.

## The plumbing is the whole point

The thing is, the wiring and the plumbing are the reason the coffee pot works. If the circuit is undersized, the breaker trips every time you run the microwave and the kettle together. If the drain was laid wrong, you find out months later when something smells. Nobody thinks about the kitchen until the kitchen fails, and then it's the only thing anyone thinks about.

APIs are the wiring and plumbing of the businesses we all depend on. AI agents are the new appliances everyone is excited to plug in. The appliances are shiny, and I get why people want to talk about them. But an appliance plugged into bad wiring is just a more expensive way to trip the breaker.

## Invisible plumbing is the goal

Look at what happened with containers and Kubernetes. For a few years those were the story. Every conference, every publication, every startup pitch. Now they are plumbing. Nobody writes breathless posts about running a container anymore. It's just how software gets shipped. That isn't a failure. That is what success looks like for infrastructure. It disappears into the walls.

APIs are heading the same direction, and in a lot of ways they're already there. Every SaaS has one. Every mobile app runs on them. Every agent is calling them. They've become so normal that they've stopped being interesting, which is exactly why they're becoming so important and so easy to neglect at the same time. Invisible is good. Invisible and unmaintained is how you get the flood.

## How to tell stories about plumbing

So how do you tell a story about plumbing to people who only care about the coffee? I've been working through this, and a few things are clear to me.

**Start with the appliance.** Lead with the outcome people want. Nobody searches for "consistent pagination across resources." They search for how to sync their orders, book a delivery, or get their agent to stop failing. I've stopped pandering to the API echo chamber. APIs are a color on the backdrop now, and the capabilities and business outcomes come first.

**Tell the story when the breaker trips.** The moment someone cares about plumbing is the moment it fails them. "My MCP server is totally wonky, what do I do?" is a plumbing question wearing an AI costume. Those are the moments to explain what's in the walls, because someone is actually listening.

**Make the inspection visible.** A home inspector doesn't lecture you about electrical theory. They hand you a report that says what passed and what didn't. That's what I'm trying to do with the Kin Score on APIs.io. It looks at the foundation, the agent readiness, and how much a company talks about AI, and puts a number on whether the wiring can handle what's being plugged into it. People will read a report card when they won't read a treatise.

**Teach the people who live in the house.** There's a growing group of people who aren't engineers but know enough to have a seat in the room. They vibe-coded a dashboard, they're loving Claude, and now they want to know what to do next. They don't need a course in electrical code. They need to know what to do and what not to do, in plain language. Bridge-building between IT and the business has always been the real work.

**Show up where they are.** Fewer people read long posts about APIs. Stories about how things work need to live in video, in podcasts, in person, and inside the AI tools people already have open. If the plumbing story can answer a question inside someone's chat window at the moment they hit the problem, that beats any post I could write.

## I'm going to keep talking about wiring

I'm not going to stop being the plumbing guy. I think telling stories about how things actually work is the future of software technology, especially now, when so much of what's being sold is designed to hide how it works. But I have to stop expecting people to come to the plumbing. I have to bring the plumbing to the coffee pot.

The best outcome for APIs is the same thing that happened to containers: they become so reliable and so boring that nobody talks about them. We aren't there yet. Right now everyone is plugging new appliances into old wiring, and the breakers are starting to trip. That's when someone in the house finally asks what gauge the wire is. I'll be there to answer.
